A recorder, and nothing else

Most tracking apps are built for runners chasing seconds, then sold to hikers. dok2 starts from the hike.

What it does

  • Records a fix every three seconds

    Fixed interval, straight from the GPS chip. Long enough to be cheap, short enough to keep the shape of a switchback.

  • Shows your position on a map with no signal

    Vector tiles read from a single map file you copied onto the phone yourself.

  • Measures climb twice

    The barometer gives you a live figure on the trail. When you stop, the track is recomputed against an on-device elevation model.

  • Marks a waypoint from the lock screen

    A spring or a junction is worth recording. It should never cost you an unlock and four taps.

  • Exports GPX to a folder you pick

    Then it stops. Move the files with Syncthing, a cable, or anything else you already trust.

What it will never do

  • social feed
  • live location sharing
  • turn-by-turn navigation
  • training plans
  • segments
  • leaderboards
  • route discovery
  • accounts
  • analytics
  • third-party SDKs
  • cloud backup

The app cannot reach the network. Not by policy — it never asks for the permission, so the phone will not let it.

Four priorities, in strict order

Every argument inside the app is settled by this list. Where two of them conflict, the earlier one wins.

01

Battery

A full day of recording should cost a small slice of the battery, and a multi-day trek should survive on a power bank.

02

Speed

From cold start to recording in under a second. No spinners anywhere in the app.

03

Privacy

No account, no analytics, no third-party code, and no permission to reach the network.

04

Accuracy — last

Deliberately. Hiking tolerates metres. That is why there is no Kalman filter, no sensor fusion, and no high-rate sampling.

The parts that are easy to get wrong

Three decisions do most of the work. All three are testable on a laptop, replayed against fixes recorded on real hikes.

Distance

Measure from the anchor, not the last fix

Walking uphill at 2 km/h moves you about 1.7 m in three seconds — under any sensible noise threshold. Compare each fix to the previous one and a whole climb reads as zero.

So the odometer keeps an anchor: the last point that actually advanced it. Slow movement accumulates against the anchor and adds up correctly. Standing still adds nothing, which is why a long lunch stop does not invent kilometres.

threshold = clamp(max(4 m, accuracy), 4 m, 30 m)

distance(anchor, fix) >= threshold
    accepted    add distance, move anchor
else
    stationary  store point, add nothing

Elevation

Barometer on the trail, terrain model at home

Satellite altitude is two to three times worse than position. Add up its wobble across a flat 10 km and the app will congratulate you on several hundred metres of climb you never did.

The pressure sensor is steadier, so it carries the live figure — calibrated against satellite altitude before recording starts, nudged gently through the day as the weather moves, and read as a median so a gust or a zipped pocket cannot book a false climb.

When you finish, the track is resampled and looked up against elevation data stored on the phone. That number replaces the live one, and it costs no battery — the hike is already over.

Battery

The GPS was never the problem

Roughly what each part of the phone draws while you record:

ComponentDraw
Screen, 6″, half brightness400–700 mW
Map drawing on the GPU150–400 mW
Cellular radio hunting for a tower100–500 mW
GPS receiver, tracking25–50 mW
Barometer< 1 mW

The screen costs about ten times the GPS chip, and a phone searching for signal in a valley can cost more than both. So the app is built to be left in a pocket, and it asks you to turn on airplane mode at the trailhead.

The missing permission is the feature

Privacy claims are cheap. This one is checkable from the phone's own settings screen, without trusting anybody.

Without the internet permission, there is no version of this app — and no update to it — that can send your tracks anywhere.

  • ✓ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION
  • ✓FOREGROUND_SERVICE_LOCATION
  • ✓POST_NOTIFICATIONS
  • ✓RECEIVE_BOOT_COMPLETED
  • ✕INTERNETnever requested
  • ✕ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATIONnot needed
  • ✕ACTIVITY_RECOGNITIONnot needed

Bringing your own map

The app cannot download anything, so you supply the terrain. Once, per region, before you leave the house.

  1. Download your region

    Grab an OpenStreetMap extract for the area you hike in from Geofabrik.

  2. Build a map file

    Run it through Tilemaker with the profile in tilemaker/. It keeps paths, tracks, contours, water, peaks and huts, and throws away everything a hiker will never look at.

  3. Copy it across and pick it

    Move the file to the phone, then choose it in Settings → Map data. A 100 × 100 km region with contours lands around 150–400 MB.

Build from source

./gradlew :app:assembleDebug
./gradlew :app:installDebug

./gradlew :domain:test   # fast, run constantly
./gradlew check          # lint + everything

The routing, filtering and elevation logic is plain Kotlin with no Android in it — so it can be tested against recorded hikes without walking up a hill.
